“Force, Fraud, and Coercion” refers to the methods of control and abuse frequently employed by traffickers to compel a likely victim to remain in the
trafficking situation. These tables group more specific methods of FFC into their overarching categories for ease of analysis:
• Economic Abuse includes, but is not limited to: taking/withholding earnings, limiting access to finances, blacklisting (or threatening to), and debts/quotas.
• Emotional Abuse includes, but is not limited to: verbal abuse or manipulation, familial-based emotional abuse, and intimate partner-based emotional abuse.
• Intimidation includes, but is not limited to: destruction of property, harming others or animals, and displaying weapons.
• Isolation includes, but is not limited to: limiting access to support systems, controlling in public, confinement, and limiting access to medical services.
• Threats include, but are not limited to: threats to harm the likely victim, their family, or others, threats to expose or shame the likely victim, threats to report
     to immigration/threats of deportation, and threats to report to law enforcement.

The data displayed in this report was generated based on information
communicated to the National Human Trafficking Hotline via phone,
email, online tip report, SMS, or Webchat. The National Human Trafficking
Hotline cannot verify the accuracy of the information reported. This is not a
comprehensive report on the scale or scope of human trafficking within the
United States. These statistics are accurate as of 07/01/2021, but are subject
to change as new information emerges or as data cleanup occurs.

Since awareness of both human trafficking and the existence of a national
victim service hotline is still limited, this data set should be interpreted as
a limited sample of actual victim or trafficking incident data, rather than a
representation of all existent victims or incidents of human trafficking. The
information reported by the Trafficking Hotline is only able to represent who
has access to and knowledge of the Trafficking Hotline, who has the means to
reach out, and who is more likely to self-identify as a likely victim or someone
in need of assistance. The data reported by Polaris should not be compared
to the findings of more rigorous academic studies or prevalence estimates.
